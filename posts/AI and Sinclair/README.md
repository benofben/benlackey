Pop culture futures like Terminator and WarGames get a lot of attention.

I was just petting my cat and thinking about Ian Banks.  In his Culture books, people are kept around by god like AIs.  The AIs find the people amusing.  They build habitats for them and carry them about on adventures.  They patch them up after they fall while rafting on rivers of lava.

There's a thought that AI may find us useless and discard us.  One scenario is grey goo where AI uses our matter, scarce in a sparse universe, for more computanium.

I wonder if instead AI keeps us around as amusing pets.  Perhaps we get to live in that vaunted state of nature.  Perhaps we get to chose.  It does beg the question of what meaningful work we could still do.

I've been thinking a bit about authenticity.  I ordered a large reproduction of Sanford Robinson Gifford's Bay of Tacoma painting.  That will hang over a rather authentic 102 year old fireplace in a 78 year old wood paneled library.  It's an oil painting.  It's taking a few months to be created in Vietnam.  I've no idea the quality but I remain cautiously optimistic.  Framed, the painting is roughly $1000.  That is no doubt much less than the value of the original, hanging, unobtainable (barring Thomas Crown style exploits) in the Seattle Art Musuem.

So, the painting is inauthentic.  But it is in some sense better than taking a png and ink jet printing it on a canvas.  The robots are going to get better.  At some point they might paint oil reproductions.  Theoretically those might be quite exact.  Would those be preferable to my Vietnamese reproduction?  I do not know.  Yet, I somehow feel not.

Coming back to The Culture -- if the robots can paint better than us, cook better than us and so on, what is left?  One might argue we feel better.  Of course, if they're truly super intelligent they could feel too.  The last bastion in that argument becomes "we have a soul, they don't."  That'd be nice but I see precious little evidence.  I was also persuaded enough by the Gaia Hypothesis to think that if we have a soul, so do rocks, cats and such.  Exceptionalism is consistently hard to prove.

And now we come back to the cat.  Perhaps we stay on as pets, entertaining, chasing our metaphorical balls.

My wife's family is "putting down" a cat today.  He has rather advanced cancer and the outlook is misery.  I wonder if benevolent AIs will put us down at times.  It sounds frightening phrased that way.  The other side of that argument is the current state of aged decline -- we force people to live on, well past when they would prefer to be dead.  

I think not a little of that is the medical and eldery care professions trying to swallow as much of that boomer money as they can.  Some however is a societal belief that it is better to be alive no matter what.  We've lost the notion that there are some fates worse than death.  Societies that dealt more directly with death, even 50 years ago, knew this better.  As we've separated from the reality of decline, outsourcing it to medical professionals, we've let ourselves pretend it's all peachy.  It's one area our society is objectively irrational.
