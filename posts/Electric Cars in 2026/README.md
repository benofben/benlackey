# Electric Cars in 2026

A friend, Matt, just visited from New York.  He flew into Portland and rented a Ford Mustang Mach-E.  The car is fully electric.  It's a somewhat cynical use of the Mustang brand that has caused much screaming from longtime Ford loyalists.

Matt went on a bit of a roadtrip, camping in the Olympics and so on before stopping at our house in Tacoma for brunch and a charge.  That did not go so well.  I believe the car was at 24% when he arrived.  Matt intended to go camping again so needed a full charge.

First off, the Ford uses a "SAE J1772 connector for Level 1 and Level 2 AC charging, and a CCS1 (Combined Charging System) plug for DC fast charging."  That is different from the North American Charging Standard (NACS) that a Tesla uses.

## Takeaways

Our charging infrastructure is gargbage.  That's widely agreed, even by proponents of electric vehicles.  We need to rebuild our electric grid regardless of whether electric cars are the path forward.  I have some more fundamental concerns however...

## Charging Time

The first issue is charging time.  In that, I suspect that electric cars are fundamentally unsuited to America.  We can change our society, retreating from the open road, the National Park roadtrip and world's largest ball of twine.  Or, we could look at ways to reduce charging time.

I suspect phsyics is a fundamental limitation here.  One workaround would be modular, removable car batteries.  You would pull up to a service station and a forklift would pull out the battery, stuffing in a charged one.  Please enjoy this fun fact:

"A Tesla battery pack weighs between 715 and 1,700 pounds (324 to 771 kg), depending on the vehicle model and its energy storage capacity (kWh). The battery generally makes up about 20% to 30% of the total weight of the car."

Modularity is roughly the solution for power tools today.  I have a small collection of $500 batteries.  When working, I shuffle them between charger and tool.  That's enough for most jobs.

Contractors I know have many many more such batteries.  While the battery is fundamentally inferior than a small ICE engine for many power tool applications (hedge trimmer, string trimmer, mower), it's also good enough.  And it's way easier to have 30 tools with 5 batteries to switch between them than attempting to keep 30 tiny ICE engines oiled and running.

Electric breaks down at scale of power consumption.  I think the line is right around a mower right now.  I'm still clinging to my ICE mower even though Honda has discontinued them in favor of electric.

## Energy Density

This gets to the question of energy density.  "Twenty gallons of U.S. regular gasoline weighs about 120 to 124 pounds."  It's a lot easier to insert 120lb of liquid into your car than 1700lb of solid.  In that, batteries are fundamentally inferior.

Solutions like hydrogen fuel cells show some promise.  They're a bit explodey, but hey, so are batteries and gas.  The hydrogen does need produced somehow though.  Presumably that could be done via fission or someday perhaps fusion.  So that issue seems surmountable.

I wonder if there's a more direct solution however.  The complex hydrocarbons in gas are amazing.  We understand the process that created them over a period of 100 million years.  I wonder if we can compress it.  Could we create a microbe or a plant that produces gas?

In the microbe model an algae like pond might grow increasingly gasoline like until it kills the microbes.  One might then distill it to usable gasoline.  Or perhaps we breed super critters that happily live in ready to burn gasoline.  The apocalypse is then triggered by their escape into the ocean, ice 9 style.

One might also imagine a gasoline fruit.  A plant makes it, bundling gasoline into a convenient package that a new style of juice bar extracts gasoline from.  Or perhaps the vehicle consumes them directly a la Mr Fusion in Back to the Future.

We're already on our way here with biodiesel.  Though, from what I understand we use more mined gas producing biodisel than we net from the process.  It only works because of poorly considered farm subsidies.

I can already hear the screams of the environmentalists -- but the carbon emissions!  When we pull carbon from the earth and release it, the emissions are positive.  But, this is something else.  We would use life to fix carbon in the atmosphere for a time.  We would then burn it. The result would be net neutral.

The process could even be taken further, producing an excess of fuel and burying the fixed carbon away.  That might prove more efficient than proposed models of growing many trees and burying them to fix carbon.  Once again, energy, in this case carbon density is key.

## Aging Out

A lot of renewable energy is terrible for the environment, hiding a polluting supply chain in a guise of respectability.  Windmills are a great example but let's pick on the cars for now.

We don't yet know how long an electric car will last.  We know the battery will age out, holding less of a charge.  When that happens, the car is likely junk.  The batteries are so heavy that they are spread throughout the car.  Removing and replacing them is complex.

Then there's the battery itself.  Disposing of that is yet another environmental hurdle.

Contrast my 2004 Toyota Tacoma with 262k miles has a much lower carbon footprint.  That footprint will likely drop further as the car edges up to the 500k miles its kin have shown themselves capable of.

## New Possibilities

There are an increasing number of human carrying drones available.  The Jetson is a neat example.  Those have very short flight times, 20 minutes or so.  Contrast even small gas planes can fly for hours.  

We have the computer control technology to build flying cars now.  If we can figure out the energy source, we might be able to do away with traffic.  If your clogged commute were suddently spread across (like the evening), traffic would cease to be an issue.

## Conclusion

I have a friend in the Bay Area, who is a rather firm eleectric disciple.  I think it's been a while since he road tripped.  I also suspect he believes road tripping slightly untoward.  The electric car works well for his short trips around the Bay Area with an occasional jaunt out to wine country.

Much of America disagrees, still driving thousands of miles for summer vacations and hundreds on a weekly basis.  It's a big country and one that's fun to explore.

I don't think lithium ion batteries are the solution here.  The energy density alone makes them untenable for long trips.  Likely alternatives I see are:

* Hydrogen fuel cells
* Synthetic gas
* Natural gas
* Growing gas (what I've described above)
